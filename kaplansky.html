Kaplansky's Crystal

In 1940 Higman, and in 1956 Kaplansky, asked about a ring built from a group. Take a group with no element that loops back to itself. Allow finite sums of its moves, with coefficients. Can a sum of several moves ever have an inverse sum? Their guess: no, only single moves can be undone.

Giles Gardam found one in 2021, with coefficients 0 and 1 where 1 + 1 = 0. Its group is a crystal group: the Hantzsche–Wendt group, generated by two screw motions of ordinary space, a(v) = (x+½, ½−y, −z) and b(v) = (−x, y+½, ½−z).

Each dot here is one element of that group, drawn where it carries a fixed point o. Its little arrow shows which way it turns space.

α is a sum of 21 group elements and α′ is another 21. Multiplying them gives 21 × 21 = 441 products. They land on only 121 group elements. Every one of those is hit an even number of times and cancels, except one: the identity. α · α′ = 1.

Over ordinary numbers the products would just pile up. The trick needs 1 + 1 = 0, which is why it lives in characteristic 2.

Every dot is computed, not drawn by hand: kaplansky.js multiplies the exact affine maps, and tests/kaplansky.test.cjs checks α α′ = α′ α = 1. This is the 2021 counterexample to Kaplansky's unit conjecture. The 2026 OpenAI counterexample to the zero-divisor conjecture lives in a random hyperbolic group far too large to draw like this.
